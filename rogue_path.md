# Rogue Path — Conceptual Design

## The two processes

A mage's etheric system degrades through two qualitatively distinct processes. Both accumulate over time, both end the character as they were, and both are available to anyone who has developed some awareness of their own etheric system: a trained mage, a warrior who noticed something without understanding it, anyone who has learned to interact with the field consciously. The etheric system doesn't distinguish intention, and it doesn't distinguish who's operating it.

**Consumption** is progressive loss of control. The etheric system stays intact, but the architecture of conscious control built through training degrades. The mage doesn't lose the ability to interact with the field; they lose the ability to do it intentionally. And the endpoint is worse than the pre-training state, not equivalent to it. A young untrained mage produces involuntary discharges from a structurally intact system with limited capacity. A mature mage in terminal consumption produces involuntary discharges from a system that has spent years learning to move large amounts of energy, with no one at the controls anymore. The training doesn't disappear; the direction over it does. The more capable the mage, the more catastrophic the loss. They become a hazard not because they want to cause harm but because they can no longer choose not to. And they know it.

**Poisoning** is progressive transformation. Energy extracted from living sources carries something the etheric system cannot process cleanly; the contamination accumulates, and the system starts to function differently, not worse. Control degrades here too, but the mage doesn't know it, because the altered state distorts their ability to evaluate their own condition accurately. They believe they're still driving. The wheel just isn't connected the way they think it is. The endpoint is someone who operates their etheric system fluently, with no awareness that the person making the decisions is no longer who they were.

Both processes can run in the same character simultaneously.

## What draining a living source means

Draining a living source produces less usable energy than equivalent ambient extraction because the energy is dirtier. The clean fraction goes to the mage; the dirty fraction goes into the etheric system as contamination. The net yield is lower, and the contamination cost is paid regardless.

The source depletes visibly. Draining something withers or incinerates it, and anyone present sees it. The social and moral weight of that depends on context.

The barrier to using this technique is narrative, not mechanical. There is no lock, no prerequisite. What stops a mage from doing it is the same thing that stops a person from killing a stranger in line at the supermarket: it's wrong, it's visible, they're not that kind of person, not yet, not unless. The circumstances that make it feel necessary are the circumstances that make it a real choice.

## Source efficiency

Not all living sources produce meaningful contamination. The relevant factor is a rough combination of mass and cognitive complexity, and the two aren't independent.

Insects, grass, small organisms: negligible contamination, negligible yield. Not worth the act.

Animals of significant size or behavioral complexity: moderate yield, light contamination. A mage with poor channel development might prefer these because they're more reactive and accessible than large stable sources.

Large ancient plants, old-growth forests: high mass, low reactivity. Stable sources that may resist extraction from an underdeveloped channel; high yield when accessible.

People and cognitively complex creatures: highest yield, highest contamination, highest moral and social weight. The sources that cost most ethically pay most energetically and cost most to the etheric system. This is not a coincidence in the system's design.

The progression is natural: a mage in crisis reaches for what's available and what they can justify. Easier sources come first. The craving that develops with advanced poisoning pushes toward higher-yield sources.

## Symptoms

Consumption and poisoning share some early symptoms, which makes diagnosis difficult and is by design. They diverge as both processes advance.

Consumption manifests as progressive loss of direction. The mage does less, controls less, reaches less. Symptoms map to capability degradation the mage can perceive and name.

Poisoning manifests as progressive transformation the mage cannot accurately perceive. Symptoms map to behavioral drift and perceptual distortion, not capability loss, and the mage's ability to read their own symptoms degrades alongside the symptoms themselves. At advanced stages the contaminated etheric system may access higher raw magnitude than a clean one, at the cost of all precision and at the cost of accurate self-assessment.

One early symptom of poisoning worth isolating: the mage begins to prefer dirty ether. The craving appears before any other obvious change, disguised as a practical preference. This is already "you are no longer yourself" expressed as a shift in taste rather than personality, and the mage reads it as a reasonable operational preference.

At advanced stages, the mage may become deliberately negligent about components. They stop maintaining supply. The absence of alternatives stops feeling like a problem.

---

## The difference that counts

The tragedy of consumption is lucid. The mage knows what's happening, watches it happen, and cannot stop it.

The tragedy of poisoning is blind. The mage believes they're fine, believes they're in control, believes the choices they're making are their own. By the time the gap between belief and reality is legible to anyone else, it's been legible for a while.

Both paths can arrive at similar mechanical states. What distinguishes them is the story of how the character got there and who they are now. There's nothing to optimize in advance, and consumption isn't even a choice — it's a consequence.

---

## Components as moral infrastructure

A mage who maintains a good component supply reduces reliance on ambient extraction and avoids the choice entirely. The system doesn't state this as a rule. It's a conclusion you reach after understanding what the alternatives cost.

Arriving in a critical situation without components is not a resource management failure. It's a question about who you are and what you'll do next.

---

## Design principle

The physics records, it doesn't judge. A mage who drains a person to save ten people accumulates the same poisoning as one who does it for convenience. The etheric system processes the act, not the intention behind it. The moral weight lives in the fiction. The mechanics produce consequences that the fiction then has to carry.
